Defining Nonfunctional Requirements

Functional requirements say what the app does. Nonfunctional requirements say how well it does it: fast, reliable, able to grow, and easy to keep running. Users rarely ask for these out loud, yet an app that is too slow or keeps breaking might as well not exist.

Case study

Social network home timelines

Book: “Case Study: Social Network Home Timelines”

An X/Twitter-style app: users post and follow each other. The main read is the home timeline, recent posts from everyone you follow. The question is whether to build it when someone reads or when someone writes.

Numbers to remember

QuantityValueHow it's derived
Posts5,800 /s500 million posts per day, on average
Peak posts150,000 /sOccasional spikes during big events
Follows200 · 200Average user follows 200 and has 200 followers. A few celebrities have 100M+.
Polling queries2 M /s10M users online, each re-running the query every 5 s
Polling lookups400 M /s2M queries × 200 followed accounts each
Fan-out writes~1.2 M /s5,800 posts × 200 followers each
Work on read

Query + polling

  • Three tables: users, posts, follows
  • The timeline is a SQL join: posts from people I follow, newest first, LIMIT 1000
  • Clients poll every 5 s to see new posts quickly
  • Cost: around 400M lookups/s, and far worse for users who follow tens of thousands of accounts
Work on write

Materialize + push

  • Keep a precomputed timeline per user, like a mailbox
  • On each post, insert it into every follower's timeline (fan-out)
  • The server pushes new posts to online followers, so no polling
  • Cost: around 1.2M timeline writes/s, still far cheaper than 400M lookups
  • Materialization means precomputing a query's result and keeping it updated. The timeline cache is a materialized view: faster reads, paid for with more work on every write.
  • During a post spike, queue the deliveries. Posts show up a little later, but timelines still load fast from the cache.
  • Following too many accounts: nobody reads all of it, so it's fine to drop some writes and show a sample.
  • Celebrity posts: dropping writes is not OK here. Store celebrity posts separately and merge them in when the timeline is read. It's a hybrid of the two approaches.
Requirement 1

Performance

Book: “Describing Performance” and “Latency and Response Time”
Users care about

Response time

  • Time from sending a request to getting the answer
  • Measured in seconds, ms or µs
  • e.g. time to load the timeline, or time until a post reaches followers
Cost depends on

Throughput

  • Requests or data volume per second, “somethings per second”
  • Each hardware setup has a maximum throughput
  • e.g. posts per second, or timeline writes per second

A system is scalable if adding computing resources significantly raises its maximum throughput.

Why response time explodes near capacity

  • Queueing: on a busy server, a new request waits for earlier ones to finish. The closer throughput gets to the maximum, the faster the wait grows.
The trap

Metastable failure

  • Long queues make clients time out, so they retry
  • Retries add even more load. This is a retry storm
  • The system can stay overloaded even after the load drops, until it is rebooted or reset
The defenses

Slow down, shed, push back

  • Client: exponential backoff with randomness, a circuit breaker, a token bucket
  • Server: load shedding (reject early when near overload) and backpressure (tell clients to slow down)
  • Queueing and load-balancing algorithms matter too

Anatomy of a response time

  • Response time is what the client sees: every delay, everywhere.
  • Service time is the time the service is actively working on the request.
  • Queueing delay is waiting for a CPU, or for outbound network buffers. It can happen at several points.
  • Latency is time the request spends latent, not being processed. Network latency is the travel time over the network.
  • Random extra delay comes from context switches, lost packets and TCP retransmits, GC pauses, page faults, and even vibration in the server rack.
  • Head-of-line blocking: a few slow requests hold up the fast ones behind them. Queueing isn't part of service time, so measure response time on the client side.
Requirement 1, continued

Mean, median and percentiles

Book: “Average, Median, and Percentiles” and “Use of Response Time Metrics”

Response time is a distribution, not one number. The mean hides how many people actually waited that long. Use percentiles to see the typical case and the slow tail.

Try it: 100 simulated requests
mean–
p50 median–
p95–
p99–

Simulated data. Each bar is one request in arrival order. Amber bars are at or above p95. Draw new samples and notice that the mean always sits above the median, because the slow outliers pull it up.

  • Mean (average): useful for estimating throughput limits, but a poor answer to “what's typical?”
  • Median = p50: half of requests are faster than this and half are slower. This is the typical wait.
  • p95, p99, p999: e.g. p95 = 1.5 s means 95 of 100 requests finish in under 1.5 s and 5 take 1.5 s or more. High percentiles are called tail latencies.
  • Jitter is variation in network delay. Outliers are the rare very slow requests.
  • Amazon targets p999 for internal services, because the slowest requests often belong to customers with the most data, who are the most valuable ones. p9999 was judged too expensive for too little benefit.

Tail latency amplification

–

  • A user request waits for its slowest backend call, even when calls run in parallel. More calls means more chances to hit a slow one: P(slow) = 1 − (1 − p)n.

SLOs and SLAs

Service level objective

SLO: the target

  • Example: median under 200 ms, p99 under 1 s
  • and at least 99.9% of valid requests return a non-error response
Service level agreement

SLA: the contract

  • Says what happens if the SLO is missed, e.g. a customer refund
  • Good availability metrics are harder to define than they look

Computing percentiles

  • Keep a rolling window, e.g. the last 10 minutes. Recompute the percentiles every minute and plot them.
  • The simple way is to sort the whole window. The cheap way is an approximation library: HdrHistogram, t-digest, OpenHistogram, DDSketch.
  • Never average percentiles across time or machines. It's mathematically meaningless. Add the histograms, then read the percentile.

Requirement 2

Reliability and fault tolerance

Book: “Reliability and Fault Tolerance”

Reliability means continuing to work correctly even when things go wrong. Working correctly means doing what users expect, tolerating their mistakes, performing well enough, and blocking unauthorized access and abuse.

One part breaks

Fault

  • A component stops working correctly: one disk, one machine, or a dependency's outage
The whole thing breaks

Failure

  • The system as a whole stops serving users, i.e. it misses its SLO
  • Fault and failure are the same event seen at different levels. A failed disk is a fault in a system that holds copies of its data elsewhere.
  • Fault-tolerant: keeps serving users despite certain faults. A part whose fault takes down everything is a single point of failure (SPOF).
  • Tolerance always has limits, e.g. up to 2 failed disks, or 1 of 3 nodes. If every node is gone, nothing helps.
  • Example: if a fan-out machine crashes, another must take over without losing or duplicating posts. That's exactly-once semantics (Chapter 12).
  • Fault injection means deliberately killing processes to keep the recovery code exercised, since many critical bugs live in error handling. Chaos engineering is the discipline built around such experiments.
  • Usually tolerate faults rather than prevent them. The exception is when there's no cure, like a security breach: leaked data can't be un-leaked.

Hardware fault rates to remember

ComponentRateSo what
Magnetic HDD2–5% / yr10,000 disks means about 1 disk failure per day
SSD0.5–1% / yrPlus about 1 uncorrectable error per drive per year, even when new. More than HDDs.
CPU core1 in 1,000Machines with a core that sometimes computes wrong results, silently
RAM (with ECC)> 1% / yrMachines hitting an uncorrectable error. Cosmic rays, defects, bit-flipping access patterns
Other partsless oftenPower supplies, RAID controllers, memory modules
DatacenterrarePower or network outages, fire, flood, earthquake, solar storms. Catastrophic if not tolerated
  • In small systems these are rare. At scale, hardware faults are part of normal operation.
  • Redundancy within a machine: RAID, dual power supplies, hot-swappable CPUs, batteries and diesel generators. It works best when faults are independent, but in practice failures are correlated (whole racks or datacenters go down).
  • The cloud approach is to tolerate whole-node loss in software rather than perfecting single machines. Availability zones group resources that are likely to fail together.
  • Bonus: rolling upgrades, patching one node at a time with no downtime (Chapter 5).

Software faults

Hardware faults

Mostly independent

  • One disk dying rarely means its neighbor dies too, at least not right away
Software faults

Highly correlated

  • Every node runs the same code with the same bugs, so they are harder to foresee and cause more failures
Five kinds of software fault Big Runaway Dependencies Interact Catastrophically
  1. Bug that hits all nodes at once: the 2012 leap second in Linux, SSDs that die at 32,768 hours
  2. Runaway process eating CPU, memory, disk, bandwidth or threads
  3. Dependency that slows down, hangs or returns corrupt data
  4. Interaction between systems with emergent behavior
  5. Cascading failure: one overload topples the next
  • These bugs lie dormant until an assumption that is usually true stops being true.
  • There's no silver bullet, only many small things: question assumptions, test thoroughly, isolate processes, let them crash and restart, avoid feedback loops like retry storms, and monitor production.

Humans and reliability

  • One study found operator configuration changes were the leading cause of outages. Hardware played a part in only 10–25%.
  • “Human error” is a symptom of a flawed sociotechnical system, not the root cause. Blaming people doesn't help.
  • Defenses: thorough tests, including property testing on random inputs, quick rollback of config changes, gradual rollouts, clear monitoring and observability, and interfaces that make the right thing easy.
  • Companies often choose features over resilience. When a preventable mistake happens, the problem is organizational priorities, not the individual.
  • Blameless postmortems: share every detail without fear, so the whole organization learns. Distrust simplistic answers, both “X should have been careful” and “rewrite it in Haskell”.
Usually tolerable

A short outage

  • A few minutes or even hours of downtime is acceptable for many apps
Catastrophic

Losing or corrupting data

  • Think of a parent's only copy of their children's photos
  • Post Office Horizon scandal (UK, 1999–2019): accounting-software bugs led to hundreds of branch managers being convicted of theft or fraud. English law presumed computers work correctly.

Cutting reliability for a prototype can be fine. Just do it knowingly.

Requirement 3

Scalability

Book: “Scalability”

Scalability is a system's ability to cope with increased load. “X is scalable” on its own is meaningless. Ask: if the load grows in this particular way, what are our options, how do we add resources, and when do we hit the limit?

  • For a startup, keep it simple and flexible. Building for hypothetical scale is at best wasted effort and at worst locks you into a rigid design.
  • Describe the load first: requests per second, GB per day, checkouts per hour, peak online users. Also its shape: read/write ratio, cache hit rate, items per user. Find out whether the average matters or a few extreme cases do.
  • Two ways to ask the growth question: same resources, more load, so how does performance change? Or same performance, more load, so how many more resources?
  • Linear scalability: twice the resources handles twice the load at the same performance. Occasionally it's better than linear (economies of scale), but usually cost grows faster than linearly.

Three architectures

Shared-memoryShared-diskShared-nothing
Also calledVertical scaling, scaling upUses NAS or SANHorizontal scaling, scaling out
ShapeOne big machine; threads share RAMMany machines with their own CPU and RAM, sharing one disk array over a fast networkMany nodes, each with its own CPU, RAM and disk. Coordination happens in software over a normal network
ProsSimpleTraditional for on-prem data warehousesCan scale linearly, uses the best price/performance hardware, scales up and down easily, survives the loss of a whole datacenter
ConsCost grows faster than linearly; bottlenecks mean 2× hardware doesn't give 2× loadContention and locking overhead limit scalingNeeds explicit sharding (Chapter 7) and brings all the complexity of distributed systems (Chapter 9)
  • Some cloud native databases have many compute nodes sharing one storage service. It resembles shared-disk, but the storage offers a database-specific API instead of a filesystem or block device, which avoids the old scaling limits.

Principles for scalability

  • There's no magic scaling sauce. 100,000 req/s of 1 kB each and 3 req/min of 2 GB each are both 100 MB/s, yet they need completely different designs.
  • Expect to rethink the architecture at every 10× in load. Don't plan more than one order of magnitude ahead.
  • Split the system into parts that work independently. This is the idea behind microservices, sharding, stream processing and shared-nothing. The hard part is deciding where to draw the lines.
  • Don't over-complicate. One machine beats a distributed setup if it's enough. If load is predictable, manual scaling has fewer surprises than autoscaling. 5 services are simpler than 50.
Requirement 4

Maintainability

Book: “Maintainability”, “Operability”, “Simplicity”, “Evolvability”

Software doesn't wear out, but requirements, platforms and dependencies change, and bugs need fixing. Most of software's cost is maintenance, not initial development. Every valuable system eventually becomes a legacy system.

  • Legacy pain: outdated technology (mainframes, COBOL), lost institutional knowledge, other people's mistakes. Maintenance is as much a people problem as a technical one.
Three principles: O·S·E. Operate it, Simplify it, Evolve it
  1. Operability: easy for the organization to keep running
  2. Simplicity: easy for new engineers to understand
  3. Evolvability: easy to change for unforeseen needs

Operability

  • Good operations can work around bad software, but good software can't run reliably with bad operations.
  • The irony of automation: the cases left for humans are the hardest ones, so more automation needs a more skilled ops team. A broken automated system is also harder to debug. More automation isn't always better.
Six ways a data system makes operations easier
  1. Visibility: metrics for monitoring, plus observability tools
  2. No single-machine dependency: take machines down for maintenance freely
  3. Docs and a clear model: “if I do X, Y happens”
  4. Good defaults that admins can override
  5. Self-healing, with manual control when needed
  6. Predictable behavior with few surprises

Simplicity

  • Big projects drift into a big ball of mud. Complexity blows budgets and hides assumptions, unintended consequences and unexpected interactions.
  • Simplicity is subjective. Which is simpler: a complex implementation behind a simple interface, or a simple implementation that exposes its internals?
  • Essential complexity comes from the problem itself. Accidental complexity comes from our tools. The split is flawed, because the boundary moves as tools improve.
  • Abstraction is the best tool: a clean facade over lots of detail, reused widely, so every improvement benefits everyone. High-level languages hide machine code. SQL hides storage structures, concurrency and crash recovery.
  • Application-level abstractions come from design patterns and domain-driven design (DDD). This book covers general-purpose ones: transactions, indexes and event logs.

Evolvability

  • Requirements always change: new facts, new use cases, new priorities, new platforms, new laws, growth.
  • Agile, TDD and refactoring help at the code level. Evolvability is that same agility at the level of a whole data system.
  • Simple, loosely coupled systems are easier to change than tightly coupled, complex ones.
  • Minimize irreversibility. A database migration you can roll back is low-stakes. One you can't is high-stakes.
