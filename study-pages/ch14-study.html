Why engineers need to think about ethics

Book: Chapter 14 introduction

Every system has a purpose and has consequences, some intended and some not. When the data is about people, we must treat it with humanity and respect. Users are humans, and human dignity comes first.

What tech is

Neither good nor bad

  • What matters is how it's used and how it affects people
  • True of a search engine just as of a weapon
  • The responsibility is ours. Focusing only on the technology isn't enough
What ethics is

Not a checklist

  • Its concepts aren't fixed; they need interpretation, which may be subjective
  • A participatory, iterative process of reflection
  • Done in dialog with the people affected, with accountability for the results
  • Guidelines exist, such as the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct, but they're rarely discussed, applied or enforced.
  • So engineers and product managers are sometimes cavalier about privacy and harmful side effects.
  • Reasoning about ethics is hard, and computing professionals rarely discuss it seriously. It's still too important to skip.
The chapter's path: Predict, then Peek
  1. Predict: using data to make automated decisions about people (bias, accountability, feedback loops)
  2. Peek: collecting the data in the first place (surveillance, consent, privacy, power, regulation)
Part 1

Predictive analytics

Book: “Predictive Analytics”

Predicting the weather or a disease outbreak is one thing. Predicting whether this person will reoffend, default on a loan or file costly insurance claims is another: it directly shapes someone's life.

The organization's view

When in doubt, say no

  • A missed opportunity costs little
  • A bad loan, a fraudulent payment or a bad hire costs a lot
  • So being cautious is rational for each organization
The individual's view

“Algorithmic prison”

  • Labeled risky (rightly or wrongly), you get no after no
  • Shut out of jobs, flights, insurance, renting, banking
  • No presumption of innocence, no proof, little chance of appeal
Three problems with predicting people: Bad Robots Repeat
  1. Bias and discrimination: learned from the past, amplified
  2. Responsibility and accountability: who answers when it's wrong?
  3. Repeat: feedback loops that reinforce themselves
Problem 1

Bias and discrimination

Book: “Bias and Discrimination”

Algorithms aren't automatically better or worse than people. But an ML system doesn't follow rules we wrote; it infers the rules from data, opaquely. If the input is biased, the output will most likely learn and amplify that bias.

  • The hope: data-driven decisions could be fairer than gut feeling and help people the traditional system overlooks.
  • The catch: even when the data shows a correlation, we may not know why.
  • Laws ban treating people differently by protected traits (ethnicity, age, gender, sexuality, disability, beliefs). But other fields can be proxies: in segregated areas, a postal code or even an IP address predicts race.
  • Expecting fair output from biased input is wishful thinking, mocked as “machine learning is money laundering for bias”.
  • Models only extrapolate from the past. A better future needs moral imagination, which only humans have. Data and models should be our tools, not our masters.

Problem 2

Responsibility and accountability

Book: “Responsibility and Accountability”

A human who errs can be held accountable, and the decision appealed. Who answers when an algorithm errs? People must not be able to hide behind the algorithm.

  • Hard questions: a self-driving car crashes; a credit model discriminates by race or religion; a judge asks you to explain how your model decided.
Credit score

“How did you behave?”

  • Based on relevant facts: your own borrowing history
  • Errors can be corrected (if not easily)
  • Bad for you, but at least about you
Predictive ML scoring

“Who is like you?”

  • Many more inputs, far more opaque
  • Judges you by similar people: stereotyping (e.g. by where you live)
  • Put in the wrong bucket, or fed bad data? Recourse is nearly impossible
  • Statistics describe crowds, not individuals. An average life expectancy of 80 doesn't mean you die on your 80th birthday. A prediction can be right on average and wrong for you.
  • Blind faith in data is dangerous. We need ways to avoid reinforcing bias, make algorithms accountable and transparent, and fix them when they err.
  • The same analytics that can target aid to people who need it can help predatory firms find vulnerable people and sell them high-cost loans or worthless degrees.
Problem 3

Feedback loops

Book: “Feedback Loops”

When a prediction changes the world it predicts, it can reinforce itself. A downward spiral, camouflaged as mathematical rigor.

LoopHow it feeds itself
RecommendationsShow people what they already agree with → echo chambers where stereotypes, misinformation and polarization grow (already visible in elections)
Credit-checked hiringBad luck → missed bills → lower score → no job → poverty → even lower score → still no job
Algorithmic pricingWhen German gas stations adopted pricing algorithms, the algorithms learned to collude: less competition, higher prices

My own made-up numbers. A good worker (score 720) loses their job in month 1 through no fault of their own. Each jobless month costs 30 points in missed bills; each employed month earns back 10. Credit-checking employers only hire above 650. Skill-judging employers hire after the bad luck ends.

  • We can't foresee every loop, but systems thinking catches many: model the whole system, people included, not just the computer parts.
  • Ask: does this system widen gaps between people (rich get richer, poor get poorer), or work against injustice? Expect unintended consequences even with good intentions.
Part 2

Privacy and tracking

Book: “Privacy and Tracking”

Beyond what we do with data, there's the ethics of collecting it. Who is the service really working for?

Explicit data

The user is the customer

  • The user typed it in because they want it stored and processed
  • The system performs a service for the user
  • Some tracking helps users too: ranking clicked results, “people who liked X also liked Y”, A/B tests
Tracked side effects

The advertiser is the customer

  • Activity logged as a by-product; the service gains interests of its own
  • Ad-funded: users' interests come second
  • Finer tracking, deeper analysis, long retention, detailed profiles for marketing
  • Users get a free service and are coaxed to engage as much as possible

The right word for that second relationship has darker connotations: surveillance.

Naming it

Surveillance

Book: “Surveillance”

A thought experiment: swap the word data for surveillance and see whether the familiar phrases still sound so good. (The book jokingly retitles itself Designing Surveillance-Intensive Applications.)

Try it: the word swap

Read it again

The example sentences are my own, in the style of startup pitch decks.

  • Trying to make software “eat the world”, we built the largest mass-surveillance infrastructure ever.
  • Soon every inhabited space has an internet-connected microphone: phones, smart TVs, voice assistants, baby monitors, toys with cloud speech recognition. Many have poor security.
  • What's new is that digitization makes collecting data about people cheap: location, relationships, messages, purchases, health. A collector may know things before you do (an illness, money trouble).
  • Past totalitarian regimes could only dream of a mic in every room and a tracker in every pocket. We accept it voluntarily because the benefits are big, and because it's corporations offering services, not governments seeking control.
Why we shrug

“Nothing to hide”

  • Only safe if you fit existing power structures, aren't a marginalized minority, and don't fear persecution
  • Or the purpose looks benign: better recommendations, personalized ads
Why we shouldn't

It decides things now

  • Cars track driving without consent → insurance premiums change
  • Health cover tied to wearing a fitness tracker
  • A smartwatch's motion sensor can reveal what you type, passwords included. Sensors and analysis only improve
“But they agreed”

Consent and freedom of choice

Book: “Consent and Freedom of Choice”

The defense: users accepted the terms, got a valuable free service, and tracking is needed to provide it. Free services are valuable, but the argument has holes.

Holes in “they consented”: Need, Know, Deal, Choice
  1. Need: is tracking needed for the feature, or only because ads pay?
  2. Know: users don't know what's collected; privacy policies obscure. Data also exposes non-users who agreed to nothing
  3. Deal: one-way extraction, no negotiation, terms set by the service. No true reciprocity
  4. Choice: opting out isn't really free
GDPR: valid consent

Freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous

  • You can refuse or withdraw without detriment, or it isn't “freely given”
  • Requests in clear, plain language, easy to access
  • Silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity are not consent
GDPR: other lawful bases

Consent isn't the only one

  • Complying with other laws; protecting someone's life
  • Legitimate interest, e.g. fraud prevention (fraudsters wouldn't consent)
  • Still, consent is the most common basis for internet services
GDPR consent: Fair Systems Inform Users
  1. Freely given
  2. Specific
  3. Informed
  4. Unambiguous

Why “just don't use it” isn't a free choice

  • A service “regarded by most people as essential for basic social participation” is effectively mandatory: smartphones, social networks, web search.
  • Network effects put a social cost on staying out.
  • Platforms are built to engage, using game mechanics and gambling-style tactics.
  • Only the privileged can opt out: people with the time to understand the policy and who can afford to miss social and career opportunities. For everyone else, surveillance is inescapable.

What privacy means

Privacy and use of data

Book: “Privacy and Use of Data”

“Privacy is dead, people post everything” is false. Privacy isn't secrecy. It's a decision right: the freedom to choose what to reveal, to whom, and what to keep secret, situation by situation.

  • Example: someone with a rare disease may gladly share medical data with researchers, but not if it could cost them insurance or a job. They need control over who sees it and why.
Before

You hold the decision right

  • You decide where you sit between secrecy and transparency
  • Part of personal freedom and autonomy
After surveillance

The company holds it

  • Privacy isn't erased; it's transferred to the collector: “trust us”
  • Results kept secret because revealing them seems creepy and hurts the business
  • Intimate facts leak indirectly, e.g. ad targeting by illness. Even unidentified, you've lost agency
  • The right is exercised to maximize profit, not by your preferences
  • Companies often manage the perception of creepiness instead of the actual intrusiveness, and do even that poorly: a factually correct reminder can reopen painful memories.
  • Expect any data to be wrong, unwanted or inappropriate sometimes, and build mechanisms for those failures. Algorithms don't know what's inappropriate unless we program respect for human needs. Be humble.
  • Privacy settings only control what other users see. The service itself keeps unrestricted access and usually grants itself broad rights to analyze internally.
  • This mass transfer of privacy rights is historically unprecedented. Surveillance used to be costly and manual; trusted relationships (doctor, lawyer) were bound by ethics and law. Online services gather sensitive data at scale without meaningful consent.
Who benefits

Data as assets and power

Book: “Data as Assets and Power”
The flattering view

“Data exhaust”

  • Behavioral data is worthless waste from using the service
  • Analytics just recycles it into value
The more accurate view

Data as labor

  • If ads pay for the service, user activity is a form of labor
  • Further: the app is bait to feed the surveillance machine; creativity and friendships are exploited
  • Proof it's valuable: secretive data brokers buy, combine, analyze and resell personal data. Startups are valued by user counts (“eyeballs”), i.e. surveillance capacity.
  • Everyone wants it: companies, and governments (via secret deals, coercion, legal compulsion or theft). Bankrupt firms sell it as an asset. Breaches are frequent.
  • So data is a toxic asset, “hazardous material”: not the new oil but the new uranium.
  • Plan for every possible future government, not just today's. “It is poor civic hygiene to install technologies that could someday facilitate a police state.” (Bruce Schneier)
  • Knowledge is power, and watching others while avoiding being watched is a key form of it. Tech companies don't seek political power, but their data still gives them power over our lives, largely outside public oversight.

A historical lens

Remembering the Industrial Revolution

Book: “Remembering the Industrial Revolution”

Data defines the information age the way industry defined the industrial one. That revolution brought long-run growth and better living standards, and also terrible harm that took a long time to rein in.

Industrial ageIts eventual safeguardInformation-age parallel
Smoke, chemical and waste pollutionEnvironmental regulationData piling up, “festering”: protecting privacy is the environmental challenge
Harsh factories, long hoursWorkplace safety rulesUsers' activity treated as unpaid labor
Child labor in minesLaws banning itPeople with no real choice to opt out
Tainted foodHealth inspectionsUnaccountable automated decisions

The right-hand column is my own mapping, as a memory aid. The book's core analogy is Schneier's: data is the pollution of the information age.

  • Safeguards raised business costs: no dumping waste in rivers, selling tainted food or exploiting workers. Society gained hugely, and few would go back.
  • Schneier's challenge: our grandchildren will judge how we handled data collection and misuse, just as we judge our ancestors on pollution. We should try to make them proud.
What to do

Legislation and self-regulation

Book: “Legislation and Self-Regulation”
GDPR: data minimization

Collect for a stated purpose

  • Collected for specified, explicit, legitimate purposes; no incompatible reuse
  • Adequate, relevant and limited to what's necessary
Big data philosophy

Collect everything

  • Maximize collection, combine with other datasets
  • Explore and experiment for unforeseen uses: the opposite of “specified and explicit”
  • The GDPR affected online advertising somewhat, but is weakly enforced and hasn't much changed wider industry culture.
  • Industry says regulation hinders innovation, and that's partly fair: shared medical data risks privacy but could improve diagnosis and save lives. Overregulation could block that. Balancing is hard.
  • The real need is a culture shift: users aren't metrics to optimize; they're humans owed respect, dignity and agency.
  • Our control over our data is like a national park: unprotected, it's destroyed, a tragedy of the commons. Ubiquitous surveillance isn't inevitable; we can still stop it.
What engineers can do: Respect, Rule, Reveal, Remove
  1. Respect: treat users as humans with dignity and agency
  2. Rule yourselves: self-regulate collection and processing to earn trust
  3. Reveal: educate users about how their data is used
  4. Remove: collect less, and purge data once it's no longer needed

If we don't consider the societal impact of our work as technologists, we aren't doing our job. (The chapter's closing point, paraphrased.)

  • Chapter 14

    Doing the right thing

    Data can help or harm: unappealable decisions, discrimination, surveillance, breaches, unintended consequences.

The final message: software and data shape the world, so engineers carry responsibility for building one that treats people with humanity and respect.
